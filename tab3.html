Part 2 · Seasonality

The spike is real — and partly measures enforcement

Sixty months, January 2021 through December 2025. Scroll to watch the Seven Dangerous Days windows shade in as the series reaches each one.

Crashes run 3.1× an ordinary day inside the Seven Dangerous Days — Songkran 11–17 April and New Year 30 December – 5 January, level with each other. Read the alcohol share with care: 5.8% of crashes inside those windows are recorded as alcohol-related against 0.8% outside — because that is when officers are running breath tests. The campaign window is when Thailand measures drink-driving, not only when it happens.

Part 2 · Forecast

Testing the reporting-lag concern

Each yearly file closes at a different time, so a month late in the year has far less time to be reported in than one early in it. If that were suppressing counts, the short-window months would come out low. Scroll for the cleanest test: the same month, five different windows.

December 2021 had 551 days to be reported and recorded 2,222 crashes. December 2025 had 28 days and recorded 2,560 — more, on a window twenty times shorter. The correlation between window length and monthly count is −0.17: negative, the opposite of what undercounting would produce, so no adjustment is applied.

Caveat: the long-window years are 2021–2022, the COVID period when traffic was already low. This rules out a large undercount, not the absence of any.

Part 2 · Forecast

Checking the forecast against April

An out-of-sample check, not a routine claim

April 2026 has now happened, so the forecast from the previous slide can be checked against it instead of only backtested. The death forecast held — 320 falls inside the 80% interval predicted before the month began, 283–422. Crashes came in just under theirs — 2,471 against 2,550–3,352 — while landing inside the 95% interval, 2,372–3,604. Both bands are drawn rather than only the one that happens to contain each result: choosing the interval after seeing the answer is what would make this check meaningless. Running it at all, and reporting it either way, is the point — a forecast nobody checks against reality is a claim, not a result.

Caveat: these are provisional counts — a snapshot reported through 6 May 2026, six days after April closed, against a source that has taken this project's other five years anywhere from two weeks to eighteen months to fully close (previous slide). A six-day window is shorter than that test covered, so the count could still rise; this is not the reporting-lag undercount already ruled out there, just an earlier read than any of the five closed years used elsewhere in this project.

Part 3 · Recommendation, part 1

Stage checkpoints at the entry points, not mid-stretch

Our recommendation, not something the crash data directly tests: place checkpoints at the on-ramps and entry points to the 14 sections above, not partway along the dangerous stretch itself. Three reasons follow from the findings already shown, rather than from a separate test of where on a stretch a checkpoint works best:

  • An entry-point checkpoint catches an impaired driver before the highest-risk kilometres, instead of only recording a crash after it happens.
  • A checkpoint mid-stretch, on a section carrying 76,000 vehicles a day on average — the measured mean traffic on these 14 sections — risks becoming a crash site of its own: vehicles braking suddenly in high-speed traffic.
  • Traffic enters a dangerous stretch from both directions, so covering both means a checkpoint at each end, not one checkpoint on one side.

Part 3 · Recommendation, part 2

How to run it, and what it cannot see

Limitation — and what follows from it

Alcohol is recorded on 1.4% of crashes, but almost entirely inside the 14 campaign days each year. Outside them it appears only when a crash was serious enough to investigate — the fatality premium falls from 2.31× to 1.17× once detection is consistent, and inverts at night. Detected year-round at the in-window rate, there would be roughly 4,900 more alcohol crashes and 750 more alcohol deaths than the 345 this data records.

Year-round breath testing is therefore a prerequisite for evaluating any deployment plan, including this one. Without it, a fall in recorded alcohol crashes cannot be told apart from a fall in testing.

Implementation: tailor by archetype, but only where the outcome licenses it. Killer highways need speed and alignment work — they kill 0.25 per crash against 0.02 on urban motorways. Urban motorways need faster incident response rather than more checkpoints — they generate half the crashes in the sample and 14% of the deaths. Freight corridors carry the largest absolute death burden, 29%, with no extreme in rate or severity, so they earn the most volume of ordinary effort, not a distinct mechanism: heavy-vehicle share does not predict severity in this data (p = 0.92). Heavy-vehicle enforcement is warranted where it was actually measured — the 14 over-crashing sections run 26% heavy against 17.8% elsewhere. Measure against the forecast's lower bound. Report deaths per 100 crashes by section, not crash counts — crash counts fall whenever traffic falls, which flatters any campaign.

Two further limits: this file is highways only — about 17% of Thailand's road deaths — and the exposure analysis covers six main routes, 23.6% of the crashes. Treat the plan as a hypothesis with a measurable test attached, not as a settled answer.
