Part 1 · Relationship

The strongest link, and a link that is not there

One point per province, all 77: crashes involving a motorcycle against crashes that do not. Scroll to fit a line through them.

They rise together almost exactly — r = +0.78, Spearman +0.71, p < 0.001. A province with more motorcycle crashes has more of every other kind too, so this is not a few places with a motorcycle problem: it scales with traffic everywhere.

And the link that is not there: weather explains 0.2% of the variation in deaths per crash (η = 0.04), and rain is the less deadly of the two large categories — 7.9 deaths per 100 against 13.1 for clear. That comparison is drawn on Tab 1's conditions carousel rather than repeated here.

Part 1 · The hook

Crash hot spots are not death hot spots

Enforcement resources get allocated by crash count. Scroll to see what happens when the same provinces are ranked by deaths instead.

Only 8 of the 12 provinces appear on both lists. Bangkok has the most crashes — 11,115 — but does not reach the top 12 for deaths. Allocating by crash count sends people to the wrong four.

Part 1 · Exposure

Traffic volume predicts how many crashes, not how deadly

160 survey sections on the six main Department of Highways routes — 26,027 crashes, 23.6% of the file. Against crash count the relationship is positive but weaker than you would expect: Pearson +0.50, Spearman +0.28, and at the same traffic volume crash counts still differ by up to 50×. Scroll to watch that trendline flatten.

Against deaths per crash the same 160 sections give −0.17 and −0.20 — reversed, and very weak. A busier road means more crashes. It does not mean a deadlier one.

Part 1 · Excess risk

Crashing more than traffic volume explains

Every one of the 160 survey sections against the crashes a traffic-volume model says it should have. The diagonal is the model being right; above it is a section crashing more than its traffic explains. Scroll to name the worst fourteen.

48 of the 160 sections out-crash the model. The worst runs 12.9× the crashes it should — Route 7 at km 4 in Chon Buri, 1,289 crashes against an expected 100. Route 9 at km 20 in Pathum Thani adds the most in absolute terms: +1,625 crashes over five years.

Five archetypes, one ranking

k-means on traffic volume, heavy-vehicle share, crash rate and deaths per crash splits the 160 sections into five kinds: freight corridors carry 29% of the deaths in this sample and killer highways 25%, while urban motorways carry 14% and kill least per crash. Ranked against a negative-binomial model that explains 48% of the variation in crash counts, the worst section runs 12.9× the crashes it should.

Part 1 · Excess risk

Five kinds of road section

k-means on four standardised variables — traffic volume, share of heavy vehicles, crash rate per distance-traffic, and deaths per crash. Every panel draws all 160 sections; each picks out one archetype. Scroll to step through them.

Freight corridors carry 29% of the deaths in this sample and killer highways 25%, while urban motorways carry 14% and kill least per crash — they crash constantly and almost nobody dies. The archetypes are named by the variable each is extreme in, never by the cluster id, which k-means assigns arbitrarily.

Heavy-vehicle share separates these groups but does not predict what happens on them: against deaths per crash it gives r = +0.008 (p = 0.92) and against crash rate −0.007 (p = 0.93), and both hold after controlling for traffic volume. We keep it because it is the only variable that makes freight corridors visible at all — on the other three they are indistinguishable from any other highway. So freight corridors lead on total deaths, not on deaths per crash: killer highways are twice as deadly each time they happen.

Part 1 · Excess risk

What the worst sections have in common

Six measures, the 14 biggest overshoots against the other 146. The grey bar in each panel is an ordinary section. Scroll to put the overshooting ones beside them.

79% of the 14 biggest overshoots are motorway — Routes 7 and 9. They carry 76,000 vehicles a day against 26,100 elsewhere and crash four times as often (33.1 against 8.3 per 100 million vehicle-kilometres), yet kill 2.1 per 100 crashes against 12.0. The worst sections share a road type; they are not a scatter of unlucky places.

The motorcycle panel is why. A motorcycle is the first vehicle in 2.7% of crashes on these sections against 8.1% elsewhere — a third the involvement, and a sixth the deaths per crash. Motorcycles are prohibited on Routes 7 and 9, and the traffic survey records no motorcycle volume at all on those eleven sections; the 1.7% of their crashes that still involve one are access and interchange traffic. The three ordinary-highway sections among the fourteen run 13.8%, where no restriction applies. So this is largely a traffic regulation showing up as a survival rate — not a claim that quieter motorcycle traffic makes a road safe. It is the same vehicle that carried 36% of deaths in Tab 1, seen from the other side.
